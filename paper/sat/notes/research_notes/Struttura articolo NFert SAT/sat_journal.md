# Smart Agricultural Technology (SAT): journal requirements and how tool + case-study papers are built there

Research date: 2026-11-07. Citation counts are OpenAlex `cited_by_count` on that date. They are confounded by age: a 2026 paper with 1 citation is not "rarely cited" in the same sense as a 2022 paper with 1.

## 1. SAT aims and scope, article types, limits, highlights, graphical abstract, data/code policy, metrics, review process

### Takeaway
SAT is the applied-technology companion to Computers and Electronics in Agriculture (COMPAG). It takes farm-scale applications of smart systems and explicitly admits "production modeling using theoretical models tested in a production environment". It is open access (APC USD 2110), IF 7.1, CiteScore 8.6, 32% acceptance, single-anonymized review with at least two reviewers. Its Guide for Authors sets no word or figure limit and lists no article types. It requires a 250-word abstract, 1 to 7 keywords and 3 to 5 highlights of up to 85 characters. A graphical abstract is encouraged but optional. Research-data sharing is only encouraged (Elsevier "Option B").

### Cited Findings
- Scope (verbatim core): SAT "provides international coverage for practical applications of smart systems that integrate advanced computing with electronic instrumentation or controls for agricultural planning and production on farm scale or in a production environment." It is "a companion to the highly reputable Computers and Electronics in Agriculture". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Technologies in scope: "algorithm development (AI), imaging, sensors integration, IoT systems, robotics, as deployed on machinery, computers or smart phones". Also: "Production modeling using theoretical models tested in a production environment will be considered." — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Application areas listed: "On-farm planning and production efficiency (crop identification, environmental impact, energy efficiency, adoption studies, socio-economic assessment) using smart technologies"; "Soil (preparation, monitoring, health management) linked to crop production"; "Seeding, growing, harvesting, pest / disease detection using own field collected data". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Peer review: "single anonymized review process". Editors screen first, and suitable papers go "to a minimum of two reviewers". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Abstract: "does not exceed 250 words". It must state the purpose, procedures, main findings with statistical significance where applicable, and conclusions, and "Emphasize new and important aspects". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Keywords: "1 to 7 keywords". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Highlights are required: "3 to 5 bullet points, each a maximum of 85 characters, including spaces", supplied as a separate editable file with "highlights" in its name. They "should capture the novel results of your research as well as any new methods". — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Graphical abstract: "You are encouraged to provide a graphical abstract at submission" (optional). It goes in a separate file, preferably TIFF, EPS, PDF or MS Office. GenAI use must follow Elsevier policy. — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Article types and length: the current Guide for Authors names no article types and sets no word, figure or table limits. It only asks for "clearly defined and numbered sections". Word files must be single-column (double-column only in LaTeX). — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Maps rule (relevant to field maps): maps "should only show the area actually studied". Do not include a location map larger than the study-area bounding box. Add the note "map lines delineate study areas and do not necessarily depict accepted national boundaries." — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Research data: "Option B" applies. Authors are *encouraged* to deposit data in a repository, cite and link it, or explain why they cannot share. Research data "may also include software, code, models, algorithms". A data statement is encouraged and is published with the article. — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Co-submission: a description of the data, methods or protocols can be co-submitted to Data in Brief or MethodsX. — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Software citation format example: "[software], Zenodo, … https://doi.org/…". CRediT roles, including "Software" and "Validation", are mandatory. — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- GenAI: a declaration is required (the statement template is given). Reviewers and editors may not upload manuscripts into GenAI tools. — [SAT Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/publish/guide-for-authors)
- Metrics shown on the journal page: CiteScore 8.6, Impact Factor 7.1, APC "USD 2110 (excluding taxes)". Timeline: 5 days to first decision, 40 days to decision after review, 69 days to acceptance, 2 days from acceptance to online publication. Acceptance rate 32%. Indexed in Scopus and DOAJ. — [SAT Journal Insights](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/smart-agricultural-technology/about/insights)
- OpenAlex: 2,684 works, 2-year mean citedness 7.13, h-index 72, ISSN 2772-3755. — [OpenAlex source API](https://api.openalex.org/sources/issn:2772-3755)
- Third-party aggregators give conflicting IF values (5.7 on journalmetrics.org, 9.86 on scijournal.org, 8.48 on Resurchify). Use the official 7.1 from ScienceDirect. — [journalmetrics.org](https://www.journalmetrics.org/journal/smart-agricultural-technology); [scijournal.org](https://www.scijournal.org/smart-agricultural-technology); [Resurchify](https://www.resurchify.com/impact/details/21101111783)
- COMPAG routes applied work to SAT: "Research applying off-the-shelf hardware or software, without augmenting such technology with investigator-developed tools, innovations, or unique approaches, should be submitted to its companion journal, Smart Agricultural Technology, whose scope includes applied technology." COMPAG also states: "Manuscripts that apply computers/electronics in an ancillary fashion or focus objectives and conclusions primarily on the application sciences (e.g., … agronomy …) should be submitted to one of those respective science journals." — [COMPAG Guide for Authors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/computers-and-electronics-in-agriculture/publish/guide-for-authors)

### Inferences
- The fastest step is the editor's desk screen (5 days), so the scope fit has to be obvious on page 1. NFert should present itself as a smart on-farm planning system: a regulatory N balance plus Sentinel-2 NNI diagnosis plus machine-ready ISOXML prescription. It should not present itself as an agronomy study, because COMPAG's scope text sends agronomy-centred objectives to agronomy journals.
- SAT's phrase "theoretical models tested in a production environment" is the hook for NFert's scope fit, and also its exposure. The 32 ha, 8-field farm is a production environment. NFert should be "tested" there, though not necessarily against yield data (see Sections 2 and 4).
- With no length limits, the length that matters is the length of comparable papers. The examples in Section 2 run to 4 or 5 numbered sections, plus ethics, CRediT, data availability and GenAI statements.
- Highlights of 85 characters are very short. Each one should hold a single number or claim, for example the "0.1 to 2.5 %" confidence range for the farm N total.

### Gaps
- Article types: none were found in the Guide for Authors text retrieved on 2026-11-07 (no software, data-paper or short-communication type mentioned). The submission system was not checked.
- Explicit review criteria or a reviewer form for SAT were not found publicly.
- The 2026 JCR value and quartile were not checked directly in Clarivate. 7.1 is the figure displayed on ScienceDirect on 2026-11-07.

## 2. Recent SAT papers (2022 to 2026) presenting a tool, DSS or pipeline with a case study

### Takeaway
SAT regularly publishes "tool + case study" papers on precision agriculture and remote sensing, and several are Italian. Most frame a twofold objective: (i) build or describe the system, and (ii) demonstrate or evaluate it on one farm or case. Validation ranges from field ground truth and yield data to usability surveys to "system-level behavioural validation" with no independent field validation. Code is rarely public: the usual data statement is "Data will be made available on request".

### Inferences
- There are two dominant skeletons in SAT tool papers:
  (A) **Software-description skeleton** (webBESyD, AgroSat): Introduction; scientific foundations and architecture; modules; implementation; test/validation; general discussion and outlook.
  (B) **IMRaD with a twofold objective** (Lanucara 2024, SugarSaT, Poss 2025, Etezadi 2026): "we developed X" plus "we evaluated or demonstrated X on case Y", then Results, Discussion, Conclusions.
  Skeleton B fits NFert better, because NFert has a genuine research finding (the correlation length controls farm-total confidence) that skeleton A would bury.
- SAT accepts single-farm demonstrations (Lanucara 2024 in Emilia-Romagna, ROAM, AgroSat). It also accepts explicitly "system-level behavioural validation rather than independent field-level validation" (Etezadi 2026), provided the limitation is stated plainly and the paper frames itself around operational behaviour, sensitivity and uncertainty. NFert's Monte Carlo soil-uncertainty analysis fits that pattern.
- Even so, every paper above has *some* external check against reality. AgroSat compares yield uniformity. webBESyD reuses published field validations of each module. Poss 2025 runs a terminal or sprayer test. SugarSaT has ground truth plus a usability survey. NFert can supply cheap equivalents without new agronomic trials:
  (i) verification against the official Fert_Office/DPI reference calculator (already in the NFert test harness);
  (ii) an ISOXML import test on an ISOBUS terminal or a validator;
  (iii) a comparison of the Sentinel-2 NNI with any available tissue or N-tester data, or a citation of published validation of the NNI–VI relationship;
  (iv) a comparison of farm totals with the farmer's actual applications or the regional reference plan.
- Code openness is rare in SAT (most papers say "Data will be made available on request"), so a CRAN or Zenodo-DOI'd R package with tests and an archived case-study dataset is a real differentiator. It deserves its own highlight and a "Code and data availability" statement.

### Gaps
- Full texts were not read end to end. Sections were taken from the HTML headings, and validation details from keyword-matched sentences. The author lists for webBESyD and some multi-author papers are truncated (first 4 authors from OpenAlex).
- No SAT paper was found that presents an *R package* specifically. Searches for "R package" and "python package" restricted to ISSN 2772-3755 in OpenAlex returned no package papers. This suggests NFert would be unusual in SAT in this respect, but it is unverified across the full corpus.

## 3. Patterns separating highly cited from rarely cited SAT and agri-tech tool papers

### Takeaway
In SAT, reviews and conceptual papers dominate the top citations (Agriculture 4.0 review, 687; Digital Twins review, 360). Among tool papers, the better-cited ones (38 to 71 citations) combine a broad, reusable framing (an IoT or fertigation framework, an FOSS spatial DSS) with real deployment or multi-season field use. Narrow single-case technical demos sit at 1 to 12 citations, but age confounds this. Independent evidence (outside SAT) shows that linking to an open data repository earns a citation advantage. Agricultural-DSS literature attributes low uptake to poor fit with users and weak credibility, which validation and transparency address.

### Cited Findings
- Top-cited SAT items are reviews and concept papers: "The digitization of agricultural industry – a systematic literature review on agriculture 4.0" (2022, 687 citations, DOI [10.1016/j.atech.2022.100042](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2022.100042)) and "Digital Twins in Agriculture: A State-of-the-art review" (2022, 360, DOI [10.1016/j.atech.2022.100094](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2022.100094)). — [OpenAlex query, ISSN 2772-3755, sorted by citations](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=primary_location.source.issn:2772-3755&sort=cited_by_count:desc)
- Tool and DSS papers, citations as of 2026-11: IoT fertigation framework 2025 (71); CWSI IoT DSS 2023, four seasons of commercial use (38); FOSS MUZ web-SDSS 2024, Emilia-Romagna (28); olive abandonment web tool 2022 (19); open-source prescription-map framework 2023 (12); SugarSaT 2025 (11); webBESyD 2025 (7); ROAM 2024 (6); ISO11783 vineyard map 2025 (5); modular fertilization DSS 2026 (2); AgroSat 2026 (1). — DOIs and OpenAlex records in Section 2.
- Data linking and citations: across 531,889 PLOS and BMC articles, statements that "link to data in a repository" were associated with "up to 25.36% (± 1.07%) higher citation impact on average". The abstract contrasts repository links with data "available on request or included as supporting information files", but it does not quantify those categories. Colavizza G., Hrynaszkiewicz I., Staden I., Whitaker K., McGillivray B. (2020), "The citation advantage of linking publications to research data", PLoS ONE. DOI [10.1371/journal.pone.0230416](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0230416). — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0230416)
- Software artifacts and citations: in 789 ICSE papers (2007 to 2017), only 58.5% of 2017 papers that developed an artifact made it public. The authors found "a small, but statistically significant, positive correlation between linking to artifacts in a paper and its scientific impact in terms of citations". Heumüller R., Nielebock S., Krüger J., Ortmeier F. (2020), Empirical Software Engineering. DOI [10.1007/s10664-020-09851-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-020-09851-6). — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1007/s10664-020-09851-6)
- Uptake of agricultural decision support tools is "disappointingly low". A UK mixed-methods study identified fifteen factors behind use, "which include usability, cost-effectiveness, performance, relevance to user, and compatibility with compliance demands". Rose D.C. et al. (2016), Agricultural Systems, DOI [10.1016/j.agsy.2016.09.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2016.09.009), 606 citations. — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.agsy.2016.09.009)
- Precision-agriculture DSSs suffer a "problem of implementation": farmers have the technology but do not use it. Participatory or user-centred design is presented as a key adoption factor, illustrated by a Swedish N-fertilization AgriDSS. Lindblom J., Lundström C., Ljung M., Jonsson A. (2017), Precision Agriculture, DOI [10.1007/s11119-016-9491-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11119-016-9491-4), 413 citations. — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1007/s11119-016-9491-4)

### Inferences
- Framing matters more than the tool. Highly cited tool papers name a general problem (resilient fertigation, management-zone delineation, water-stress irrigation) rather than a product. For NFert, lead with the general question: "how confident can a farm be in a regulatory N total computed from digital soil maps, and what controls that confidence?" The answer (spatial correlation length of the soil error, 0.1 to 2.5%) is transferable to any DSM-driven nutrient-balance tool in Europe. Present the Emilia-Romagna DPI 2026 implementation as the testbed.
- Reusability drives citations. An open R package with a reproducible case study and archived data (Zenodo DOI) gives other groups something to cite when they reuse it. This is rare in SAT (Section 2).
- Single-case, region-specific regulatory tools risk low citation unless they generalise. The paper should explicitly map which parts generalise (NNI-to-prescription, ISOXML strips, DSM Monte Carlo) and which are DPI-specific (coefficients, rules).
- The finding that operational deployment boosts citations (CWSI DSS, four seasons) suggests that even modest use evidence, such as the farm's real application or an advisor's feedback, would strengthen the paper.

### Gaps
- No bibliometric study was found that is specific to SAT or COMPAG tool papers and isolates the effect of validation or code openness on citations. The patterns above are inferences from a small, age-confounded sample.
- Semantic Scholar's API rate-limited the session (HTTP 429), so citation counts are OpenAlex only and may differ from Google Scholar or Scopus.

## 4. Do SAT, COMPAG, Precision Agriculture or EMS reject tool papers lacking validation? Editorial guidance on software papers

### Takeaway
No editorial was found stating that SAT desk-rejects tool papers without field validation, and SAT has published a 2026 nitrogen DSS that openly reports only "system-level behavioural validation". The journals' own scope texts do, however, set expectations that NFert must meet. SAT wants models "tested in a production environment". COMPAG demands novelty and routes off-the-shelf applications to SAT. EMS requires software papers to report "substantial testing and evaluation", quantitative verification and validation, comparisons with alternatives, adoption, and licensing. The established standard of model-evaluation good practice is Jakeman et al. (2006).

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The main risk for NFert at SAT is a reviewer asking "is the prescription agronomically better?", not a desk rejection. Mitigations:
  (1) Frame the contribution as a verified implementation plus uncertainty quantification plus operational output, not as an agronomic yield gain.
  (2) Report verification quantitatively: agreement with Fert_Office/DPI reference outputs across all test cases, plus unit-test coverage. Under the EMS criteria this counts as "verification" backed by numbers.
  (3) Report a behavioural or sensitivity analysis (the Monte Carlo and correlation-length result) in Etezadi-style language.
  (4) Show that the ISOXML/strip output is machine-readable, as in Poss 2025.
  (5) Add a dedicated "Limitations and validation roadmap" subsection stating that there is no independent agronomic validation yet, and what trial would provide it.
- Following the EMS scope checklist, even for SAT, pre-empts reviewer objections: clear objectives, rationale, testing, comparison with alternatives (for example uniform DPI rate vs. VR, or deterministic vs. Monte Carlo farm total), licensing and open-source access, and intended users and adoption path. Comparing against an alternative approach is the cheapest way to add a "benchmark".
- Avoid presenting the farm as "validation". Call it a "case study" or "demonstration", as AgroSat, ROAM and Lanucara 2024 do. Reserve "validation" for the reference-calculator verification.
